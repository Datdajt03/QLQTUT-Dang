Edge Artiicial Intelligence (AI) allows machine learning models to run directly on resource-constrained IoT and edge
devices instead of relying only on cloud resources. This shift reduces latency and improves privacy but introduces strict
constraints on memory, energy, and connectivity across heterogeneous sensor nodes. Deploying AI on constrained devices
also requires addressing fragmented toolchains and inconsistent evaluation practices. This survey provides a structured review
of deployment methods for edge AI, including model compilation, intermediate representations, hardware-aware optimization,
scheduling strategies, and connectivity-aware execution. Post-deployment aspects are also examined, including lifecycle
management, secure over-the-air updates, and benchmarking frameworks that combine latency, accuracy, and energy. Use
cases in healthcare, smart cities, autonomous systems, manufacturing, and agriculture illustrate practical applications. Across
these areas, common challenges in portability, reproducibility, adaptivity, and security are identiied, along with emerging
directions such as dynamic orchestration across heterogeneous devices and modular update mechanisms. By combining
perspectives from compiler design, system optimization, and lifecycle management, this survey highlights how deployment
pipelines are evolving into key enablers for reliable and scalable intelligence at the network edge.

Introduction

are shifting toward the edge of the network, the physical locations where data is generated. This transition,
commonly referred to as edge AI, is transforming the way intelligent systems operate by enabling low-latency
processing, enhancing data privacy, and reducing bandwidth requirements. Edge AI is being adopted across
various domains, including industrial automation, healthcare, smart cities, and autonomous systems [39, 226, 286].
At the hardware level, this transformation covers a diverse spectrum of IoT platforms. These range from
ultra-constrained microcontrollers (MCUs) to system-on-chip (SoC) devices and single-board computers (SBCs)

While these studies cover important aspects such as optimization techniques, TinyML pipelines, and deployment
toolchains, they typically focus on the design and execution of models up to the inference stage. In contrast, post-
deployment operation and long-term maintenance, such as Over-The-Air (OTA) updates, runtime monitoring, and
lifecycle adaptation, remain largely unaddressed. To make this distinction explicit, we categorize contributions
along three phases of the deployment lifecycle: pre-deployment (data preparation, architecture design, model
training, and optimization), deployment (compilation toolchains, hardware mapping, and scheduling), and
post-deployment (monitoring, OTA updates, runtime adaptation, performance evaluation, and benchmarking).
Despite growing interest in edge AI, the post-deployment operation of models in the ield remains underrepre-
sented. Beyond optimizing models for inference, production systems must remain maintainable, updatable, and
observable. Prior work highlights that many edge deployments rely on static, pre-trained models that seldom
adapt after deployment [188], and that on-device deployments face substantially more complex update and
maintenance burdens than cloud-managed systems [257]. Moreover, operational requirements such as model
version management, runtime monitoring/telemetry, and safe update worklows are central to real-world edge
deployments [137]. These needs are particularly acute in safety- or mission-critical domains, such as healthcare
and industrial automation, where reliability lapses or outdated models can pose signiicant risk [232, 258].

In recent years, the ecosystem of deployment tools has expanded rapidly. Modern pipelines now encompass
model-level optimization, graph transformations at the compiler level, hardware-aware code generation, and
runtime management tools for deployment and monitoring. These solutions aim to bridge the gap between AI
research and real-world embedded systems. Yet the landscape remains fragmented: developers and practitioners
must often navigate inconsistent hardware support, opaque performance trade-ofs, and a lack of uniied, scalable
deployment worklows [86, 137].
While edge AI emphasizes local inference and autonomy, many real-world applications require a careful balance
between edge and cloud processing. In domains such as wearable health monitoring [232, 258] or predictive
maintenance in industrial IoT [188, 232], strictly on-device operation may be essential due to latency, connectivity,
or privacy constraints. In contrast, other scenarios beneit from cloud assistance for tasks such as model updates,
coordination, or compute-intensive inference [286].
At the same time, the demand for reliable and maintainable edge AI deployment continues to grow. According
to IDC, worldwide spending on edge computing is projected to rise from about $261 billion in 2025 to nearly
$380 billion by 2028, driven by applications that require real-time intelligence, data privacy, and autonomy at the
edge [109]. Developing robust, scalable deployment tooling is therefore a key enabler for the next generation of
AI applications across sectors.
Scope of the Survey. Figure 1 illustrates the three principal phases of the edge AI deployment lifecycle considered
in this survey: pre-deployment covering data acquisition and preparation, model design and optimization;
deployment covering compiler toolchains, hardware integration, and connectivity-aware worklows; and post-
deployment covering in-ield monitoring, over-the-air updates, and lifecycle management. The igure also maps
representative tools and frameworks used in each phase, highlighting how the current ecosystem covers the
complete edge AI worklow from data processing to long-term maintenance.
This survey, centered on literature from 2020-2025 and supported by earlier foundational work for context,
aims to address the gap in deployment-focused literature. The speciic contributions are as follows:
(1) Scope and Taxonomy: We deine the scope of edge AI deployment for IoT platforms, covering heteroge-
neous devices from ultra-low-power MCUs to high-performance SBCs. A taxonomy of deployment tools is
presented, spanning the AI lifecycle from model design to long-term maintenance.
(2) Pre-deployment and Deployment Worklows: We provide a comprehensive review of both academic
frameworks and commercial toolchains, analyzing model optimization, compiler pipelines, hardware
integration, and connectivity-aware worklows.
(3) Post-deployment Lifecycle: We survey approaches for runtime monitoring, OTA updates, and lifecycle
management, highlighting their importance for reliability and maintainability in constrained environments.
(4) Evaluation and Benchmarking: Where available, we compare tool capabilities and discuss trade-ofs in
latency, memory footprint, energy eiciency, and deployment portability.
(5) Applications: We illustrate practical relevance through application domains such as industrial monitoring,
medical devices, and autonomous systems, showing how deployment complexity afects real-world use.
(6) Research Challenges and Open Research Questions: We identify challenges and open research ques-
tions in modularity, usability, lifecycle management, deployment adaptivity, and security, and outline future
directions for scalable, maintainable, and trustworthy edge AI deployment.

Conceptual Foundations: 1990s and Early 2000s. In the 1990s, the concept of moving computation closer to the
physical world began to emerge, as researchers explored how intelligent systems could interact more directly
with their environments. One important contribution was Mark Weiser’s vision of ubiquitous computing, which
proposed that computation would become seamlessly integrated into everyday objects and environments [261].
A few years later, Schilit et al. introduced the concept of context-aware computing, where systems adapt their
behavior based on contextual information such as location, nearby devices, and user activity. The term "Internet of
Things" is attributed to Kevin Ashton, who introduced it in 1999 during a presentation at Procter & Gamble [169].
The concept was subsequently popularized by Gershenfeld, Krikorian, and Cohen in their Scientiic American
article "The Internet of Things" [80]. Cisco later quantiied the practical emergence of IoT as the point when the
number of connected devices surpassed the number of people, estimating that IoT was łborn” between 2008
and 2009 [72]. These early developments, while not directly involving AI, established the foundational idea of
pushing intelligence closer to the physical world, an idea that would later be enabled by advances in machine
learning and eicient edge computing.
Early Edge Intelligence and Local Processing (2011-2014). Building on earlier visions of ubiquitous and context-
aware computing, the early 2010s saw research and industry begin exploring architectures to bring computation
closer to data sources, aiming to complement cloud computing with localized capabilities at the edge. Fog
computing, introduced by Flavio Bonomi at VANET 2011 and formalized in 2012 [36], proposed a distributed
layer between cloud and IoT devices, enabling geo-distributed, low-latency processing for real-time applications
such as connected vehicles, smart grids, and sensor networks.

Industry initiatives like the Embedded Vision Alliance (2011) promoted the adoption of computer vision
in embedded and resource-constrained products [68]. Meanwhile, ETSI launched the Mobile-edge Computing
(MEC) industry initiative in 2014, later renamed Multi-access Edge Computing [71], to standardize application
deployment at the mobile network edge, with use cases spanning IoT, video analytics, augmented reality, and
local content distribution [70].
At the same time, conceptual frameworks such as the Wisdom Web of Things (2012) [87] envisioned augmenting
IoT with embedded intelligence. Together, these developments marked the shift from connected IoT systems
toward localized, intelligent processing at the network edge.
Enabling AI at the Edge (2015-2018). Between 2015 and 2018, advances in hardware, software, and algorithms
transformed edge AI from architectural vision to practical deployment on IoT devices. Dedicated platforms such
as NVIDIA’s Jetson TX1 (2015) [75] pioneered GPU-accelerated inference for computer vision in constrained
environments, while the Raspberry Pi 3 (2016) [1] democratized prototyping by providing an accessible, low-cost
platform.
In parallel, lightweight frameworks bridged cloud-trained models with edge deployment. TensorFlow Lite
(2017) [3], now renamed as Lite Runtime (LiteRT), introduced model quantization and optimization for mobile
and edge devices, while the Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX, 2018) [2] enabled portability across
heterogeneous hardware. On ultra-constrained platforms, ARM’s CMSIS-NN (2018) [134] brought eicient neural
kernels to Cortex-M microcontrollers, extending AI to battery-powered IoT sensors.
The period also saw the emergence of specialized accelerators. ARM’s Project Trillium (2018) [4] and Google’s
Edge TPU (2018) [53] delivered NPUs optimized for embedded inference. Meanwhile, eicient ML techniques
such as the łHello Edge” keyword spotting system [277] demonstrated that deep learning could run within 100
KB of memory, laying the foundation for the TinyML movement. Together, these developments established the
technical feasibility of practical, localized AI across the edge spectrum.
Ecosystem Growth and First Mature Deployments (2019-2020). By 2019, edge AI had moved from feasibility to
broader ecosystem adoption. NVIDIA’s Jetson Nano [5] provided afordable GPU-enabled inference for makers
and researchers, lowering the barrier to prototyping computer vision and robotics applications at the edge.
At the same time, Google’s TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers [207] extended deep learning to bare-metal
microcontrollers with only kilobytes of memory, enabling on-device inference for ultra-constrained IoT sensors
and marking a milestone for the emerging TinyML community.
In parallel, practical deployments began to validate the edge AI vision. Google’s Gboard integrated federated
learning to train language models directly on smartphones, demonstrating privacy-preserving distributed train-
ing at a global scale [35]. Meanwhile, the emergence of 5G networks provided high-bandwidth, low-latency
infrastructure to complement local inference [235]. Together, these developments marked the transition from
isolated prototypes to deployable edge AI systems across diverse IoT devices.
Industrialization and Full-Stack Maturity (2021-2025). From 2021 onward, edge AI entered a phase of industrial
consolidation, deined by the integration of hardware, toolchains, and lifecycle support into production-grade
worklows. Unlike earlier stages centered on individual components, this period emphasized full-stack systems
spanning model optimization, deployment, and long-term maintenance.
On the hardware side, system-on-chips increasingly embedded neural accelerators, exempliied by Google’s
Tensor SoC (2021) for on-device speech, vision, and translation [83]. At the ultra-constrained end, ARM Cortex-
M microcontrollers and low-power SoCs enabled practical inference in health monitoring, voice control, and
predictive maintenance [24], extending applicability across both high-end and deeply embedded devices.

development worklows, and integration across hardware platforms and application domains.

Edge-Cloud Trade-ofs and Connectivity Considerations
The growing proliferation of IoT devices and the increasing demand for real-time AI applications have catalyzed
a shift toward distributed intelligence at the network edge. While cloud computing ofers virtually unlimited
computational power and centralized data management, relying solely on cloud resources introduces signiicant
limitations in scenarios that require low-latency responses, strong privacy guarantees, or operate under bandwidth
constraints [286].
Pushing AI workloads closer to data sources, a concept often referred to as edge AI (or Edge Intelligence, EI),
seeks to address these constraints by executing inference and, in some cases, parts of the training process at edge
nodes [232, 286]. On-device inference provides latency beneits and improves data privacy by reducing external
transmissions. However, the constrained compute and memory resources of edge devices impose strict limits on
model size and complexity, often necessitating compression techniques such as quantization and pruning [59, 222].
Conversely, cloud-based inference enables more sophisticated models but sufers from network-induced latency
and reliability issues. Transmitting large volumes of raw sensor data to cloud datacenters is often infeasible
in terms of bandwidth cost and response time, particularly in applications like autonomous vehicles, smart
surveillance, and industrial monitoring [59, 286].
To bridge this trade-of, hybrid edge-cloud architectures have emerged as a prevalent solution, where workloads
are dynamically partitioned based on resource availability, latency requirements, and network conditions [232].
Hierarchical computing models further distribute computation across devices, edge servers, and cloud datacenters
to balance workload, data locality, and energy eiciency [216]. The taxonomy of edge intelligence levels introduced
by Zhou et al. [286] explicitly categorizes systems based on the degree of computation performed at the edge
versus the cloud, highlighting the absence of a single solution.
Connectivity variability further complicates deployment strategies. Fluctuating bandwidth and intermittent
links in remote deployments mandate adaptive oloading strategies [222]. Meanwhile, the need for privacy-
preserving computation (e.g., in healthcare or defense applications) reinforces the importance of context-aware

Performance Constraints, Trade-ofs, and Metrics
Deploying AI models on edge devices requires careful consideration of performance constraints and trade-ofs
that are often more restrictive than those encountered in cloud or desktop environments, as highlighted both by
benchmarking eforts such as MLPerf Tiny [24] and by recent survey studies [226].
Key performance metrics for edge AI include inference latency, typically measured in milliseconds or frames
per second, which is critical for real-time responsiveness [24, 232]. Energy consumption is another central metric,
often quantiied as millijoules per inference, particularly in battery-powered devices such as wearables and
remote sensors [86]. Model accuracy remains essential, although some loss may be acceptable when optimizing
for latency or energy [232]. Memory usage, encompassing both RAM and on-device storage, directly constrains
deployability on microcontrollers and other low-resource devices. Trade-ofs are inevitable: improvements in
accuracy typically incur higher latency and energy consumption on resource-constrained devices [86]. Improving
throughput on edge often relies on model compression and hardware-aware tuning, which boost speed but can
reduce model size and lock deployments to speciic accelerators, limiting portability [232].
Diferent applications emphasize diferent metrics: wearables prioritize energy eiciency and minimal memory
footprint [137], drones and robotics demand ultra-low-latency inference for real-time control loops [226], while
industrial systems often balance accuracy with continuous uptime under noisy conditions [86]. These trade-ofs
are revisited in Section 5.3, where latency, energy, accuracy, memory footprint, and model size are discussed as
interdependent constraints rather than isolated metrics.
One persistent challenge is the lack of standardized benchmarks that fully capture the operational constraints
of edge and embedded devices [24, 137]. Inference performance cannot be fully described by model size alone;
it must account for latency, memory access patterns, energy eiciency, and real-time responsiveness [232].
Benchmarking methodologies are discussed in detail in Section 5.4.

and bottom-up connections. These design choices allow EicientDet to balance accuracy and latency more
efectively than prior detectors, making it competitive on mobile and embedded platforms.
More recent designs, such as YOLOv10n [252] and YOLOv11n [124], further reine detection heads and scaling,

3.3
Model Compression and Deployment-Oriented Optimization
Model compression techniques and deployment-oriented optimization methods focus on adapting a trained
model to the strict resource constraints of edge devices. Rather than changing the learning task or data, these
techniques reshape the inal artifact to reduce latency, memory, and energy consumption, while striving to
preserve accuracy for eicient on-device inference. A key enabler is the integration of such methods into widely
adopted deployment toolchains, which makes them practical for real-world embedded AI.
Post-Training Quantization (PTQ). Post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces weights and activations to low
precision, typically 8-bit integers, thereby decreasing model size and improving inference latency on constrained
hardware [115]. Because PTQ requires no retraining, it is supported across nearly all major toolchains, LiteRT,
ONNX Runtime, OpenVINO, and vendor SDKs, making it one of the most accessible techniques for practitioners.
However, standard PTQ can cause accuracy degradation, especially in compact models or sensitive tasks. Recent
reinements mitigate this efect. For example, BRECQ reconstructs intermediate outputs during calibration [141],
SmoothQuant rebalances activations and weights for large models [266], and PTQ4SAM adapts the method to
segmentation transformers [156]. Comprehensive studies conirm these trends: Gholami et al. survey quantization
methods across architectures [81], while Yao et al. show that ine-grained PTQ enables eicient compression even
for billion-parameter LLMs [271]. In practice, PTQ ofers a favorable trade-of between eiciency and accuracy,
and its broad tool support ensures straightforward deployment on edge accelerators.
Quantization-Aware Training (QAT). Unlike PTQ, which applies quantization post hoc, QAT integrates low-
precision arithmetic directly into the forward and backward passes so that parameters adapt during training [115].
QAT typically achieves higher accuracy than PTQ, staying within 0.2-0.5% of full-precision baselines at 8-bit,
while also delivering measurable latency gains on embedded GPUs such as the Jetson Nano [233]. Support for
QAT is now provided in toolchains such as TensorFlow Model Optimization Toolkit (TFMOT), PyTorch QAT, and
ONNX Runtime QNN, which embed hardware-aware quantization schemes directly into training worklows.
The main drawbacks are higher training cost and reduced portability when the simulated quantization scheme
difers from backend support [50, 171]. Zhao et al. introduce the notion of recoverable accuracy to quantify when
QAT is necessary, showing that it is most beneicial at very low precision (e.g., INT4, INT2) or in layer-sensitive
settings [280]. These insights have motivated selective-QAT approaches, increasingly integrated into frameworks
like TVM Unity and OpenVINO, which allow hybrid precision deployment with reduced retraining burden.

partially trained networks. Sparse training typically employs sparsity-inducing regularizers such as weight decay

Federated Learning (FL). FL trains a shared global model by aggregating updates from distributed devices
rather than centralizing raw data, thereby preserving privacy and reducing bandwidth usage in domains such
as healthcare and IoMT [240]. Widely adopted algorithms include FedAvg, with extensions such as FedProx

challenges remain: frequent synchronization rounds can impose high communication overhead on wireless

Compilation Pipelines and Intermediate Representations
Compilation pipelines transform trained models from high-level frameworks (e.g., TensorFlow, PyTorch) into
deployment-ready binaries tailored to speciic hardware targets [49]. Most modern toolchains introduce an
intermediate representation (IR) layer, e.g., ONNX (exchange IR), MLIR (multi-dialect compiler IR), or Relay

heterogeneous accelerators and easing integration with inference engines [2, 49, 136].
In many deployment stacks, the IR is explicitly exposed alongside the runtime engine, supporting both hardware
mapping and lifecycle integration [78]. In accelerator-oriented compilers, the IR also provides a substrate for
memory-constrained placement and datalow mapping under strict on-chip resource limits [118].
Representative toolchains illustrate the diversity of IR-based deployment. TensorFlow Lite Converter trans-
forms TensorFlow models into FlatBufer-encoded binaries and supports optimizations such as post-training
quantization [199]. ONNX exporters and ONNX Runtime enable cross-framework portability through the ONNX
exchange format and backend-speciic Execution Providers [10, 116]. Apache TVM compiles models to optimized
kernels on heterogeneous targets and applies graph-level transformations via Relay; in its newer Unity stack,

OpenVINO, inally, is a framework-agnostic inference engine optimized for Intel hardware, with limited support
for graph-level optimization [151]. In contrast, Tinygrad is an open-source framework that combines model
deinition, automatic diferentiation, and a compact compiler and runtime supporting CPU, GPU, and OpenCL
execution. It provides a simpliied example of integrated deployment functions [241].
Beyond open-source toolchains, commercial ecosystems increasingly embed IR worklows into end-to-end
deployment pipelines. For example, NVIDIA Triton integrates TensorRT engines into both Jetson-class devices and
cloud services, illustrating how IRs can serve as the foundation of uniied edge-cloud execution [217]. Qualcomm
AIMET extends this idea to mobile platforms by combining IR conversion with compression and quantization for
Snapdragon NPUs. Meanwhile, cloud providers such as AWS Greengrass and Microsoft Azure Percept expose
IR-based deployment lows, but these remain tightly coupled to their proprietary infrastructures [119]. Taken
together, these examples show that intermediate representations are increasingly used as integration points
linking device optimization with lifecycle management. In parallel, microcontroller ecosystems have evolved
to address the speciic constraints of embedded platforms. STMicroelectronics provides a parallel deployment
environment for MCU-class devices through STM32Cube.AI, an integrated compiler and proiler that converts
trained neural networks into optimized STM32 irmware, supporting quantization, static memory planning, and
runtime proiling. Complementing it, the STM32 AI Zoo ofers a curated collection of pre-optimized reference
models, datasets, and deployment examples targeting STM32 MCUs and MPUs. These tools are examples of
vendor-speciic ecosystems that connect standard machine-learning frameworks with practical deployment on
embedded microcontrollers [229, 230].

such as operator fusion and constant folding reduce redundant work and improve memory locality, while
systematic infrastructures like Apache TVM and MLIR make it straightforward to implement these and related
transformations [49, 136].
Beyond generic passes, frameworks increasingly rely on hardware-optimized kernel libraries provided by
vendors or open-source communities. Widely used examples include CMSIS-NN for Arm Cortex-M devices,
cuDNN for NVIDIA GPUs, and XNNPACK for mobile CPUs. By mapping high-level operators to such kernels,
compilers achieve large performance gains with minimal developer efort. Mainstream deployment stacks such as

generic graph optimizations with hardware-speciic acceleration layers [2, 59].

TensorFlow Lite Micro baselines while still meeting these thresholds across embedded platforms [24].

local execution and cloud oloading. At a higher level, device-edge-cloud pipelines and adaptive partitioning
have been widely investigated in the edge-intelligence literature, yet these capabilities are not integrated into
commercial toolchains.
Industrial ecosystems emphasize deployment, integration, and monitoring rather than ine-grained parti-
tioning. For instance, NVIDIA Triton provides inference-side scheduling (dynamic batching and concurrency
management), enabling scalable service orchestration [217]. AWS IoT Greengrass [6] and Azure IoT Edge [7]
similarly focus on modular container deployment and cloud integration, with support for running ML workloads
at the edge, but do not ofer irst-class support for connectivity-adaptive partitioning.
Overall, Section 4 shows that deployment support is more mature at the device-optimization level than at the
system-adaptation level. As summarized in Table 7, compilers, IRs, and hardware-aware runtimes increasingly
improve execution on heterogeneous devices, while connectivity-aware partitioning and lifecycle integration
remain less consolidated in mainstream toolchains. The main challenge is therefore to move from isolated
optimization tools toward deployment worklows that jointly support portability, specialization, and adaptation.

Post-Deployment Phase, Evaluation Criteria and Benchmarking
The post-deployment phase in edge AI covers the operational lifetime of deployed models. In contrast to compila-
tion and deployment, which focus on producing a working artifact, this phase emphasizes long-term reliability,
adaptability, and security. Efective post-deployment strategies ensure that models continue to perform robustly
under dynamic environmental conditions, shifting data distributions, and evolving application requirements.
This is particularly important in distributed edge deployments, where leets may consist of thousands of resource-
constrained devices, making manual updates impractical and necessitating automated monitoring and OTA
updates [28].
In addition to lifecycle management, rigorous evaluation and benchmarking are central to sustaining reliable
operation. Metrics such as latency, accuracy, memory footprint, and energy consumption deine the performance
envelope of deployed systems, while standardized benchmarks provide the means to compare solutions across
heterogeneous hardware and software stacks. Taken together, lifecycle mechanisms and evaluation frameworks
form the foundation for trustworthy post-deployment operation of edge AI.

5.1
Lifecycle and Monitoring
Model Versioning and Rollback. Maintaining consistency across large, heterogeneous leets of edge devices
requires disciplined version control, authenticated update mechanisms, and robust rollback support. Versioning
schemes combined with cryptographic signatures provide a foundation for integrity and authenticity, while
gradual or staged deployment strategies are widely recommended to mitigate the risk of system-wide failures.
A frequently adopted technique to realize reliable update deployment is dual-bank (A/B) partitioning. In this
approach, the new irmware image is irst staged in an inactive partition and activated only after successful
veriication. If validation fails, the system automatically reverts to the previous stable image, thus minimizing
downtime and ensuring recovery from faulty or malicious updates. This rollback-capable design has become a de
facto standard in resilient IoT and edge platforms [18]. Surveys note that manual intervention does not scale,
making automated rollback a key requirement for IoT and edge maintainability in IoT and edge deployments [67].
Sorensen et al. evaluated diferential irmware updates on embedded IoT leets using an A/B dual-partition setup
with rollback support, showing that eiciency gains can be achieved while still allowing the device to automatically
fall back to its previous stable irmware if an update fails [227]. Complementary eforts on runtime partial updates
for microcontrollers have achieved near-zero downtime, with only millisecond-level interruption, by replacing
targeted code segments during execution. The proposed method leverages temporary shadow bufering for
veriication, allowing updates without reboot while preserving ongoing application functionality [174]. Large-
scale empirical analyses of commercial irmware update systems continue to reveal vulnerabilities such as
incomplete version checks and missing freshness guarantees. These weaknesses enable downgrade and rollback
attacks, demonstrating that laws in the update logic can undermine device security even when other protective
mechanisms are in place [263]. In response, security-by-design OTA frameworks have been proposed, embedding
modular cryptographic protections, explicit rollback policies, and lifecycle governance directly into the update
pipeline [114].

performance signals, input- and prediction-data statistics, outlier detection, and drift detection to identify degraded

5.2
OTA Updates and Model Adaptation
Secure and Eicient OTA. Over-the-air mechanisms enable remote patching and upgrading of models and
irmware without requiring physical device access, which is essential for managing large, geographically dis-
tributed leets. Security is typically enforced through lightweight public-key cryptography. Recent evaluations
conirm that elliptic-curve signatures are practical even on microcontrollers, providing equivalent security with
much smaller key sizes and reduced computational and energy overhead compared to RSA [192]. Complemen-
tary work has demonstrated the feasibility of stateful hash-based schemes, such as XMSS and LMS, on ARM
Cortex-M devices, with optimized implementations achieving signing and veriication within a few hundred
milliseconds and itting comfortably within tens of kilobytes of RAM, providing forward-secure alternatives for
update authentication under resource constraints [44].
Eiciency is achieved through techniques such as delta encoding, block-level compression, and chunk-based
streaming. Empirical evaluations show that diferential update schemes can reduce transmission overhead by
nearly half and installation time by up to 78%, while preserving rollback safety in embedded deployments [227, 260].
Building on this, hybrid distribution and scheduling strategies have been proposed to balance bandwidth and
latency in large-scale IoT and automotive environments.
Beyond transmission and installation eiciency, secure OTA worklows should also quantify the resource
overhead introduced by security mechanisms. We refer to this as security overhead: the additional latency and
energy consumption, as well as memory usage, storage footprint, transmitted bytes, or installation time, caused
by mechanisms such as signature veriication, integrity checking, authenticated manifests, encrypted payload
handling, and rollback safeguards. These overheads depend on the update mechanism, network technology,
and device class [66]. Recent work on LoRaWAN irmware updates further shows that latency and energy
are central evaluation dimensions for constrained wireless deployments, especially for time-sensitive security
patches [37]. RIOT-ML provides partial support for such evaluation by measuring memory, storage, manifest, and
network-transfer costs for SUIT-based secure TinyML OTA updates across diferent update granularities [103].
However, RIOT-ML does not deine security overhead as a uniied criterion and excludes SUIT payload encryption
from its measurements. Future OTA evaluations should therefore report security overhead explicitly, particularly
for encrypted model updates on constrained devices.
At the system level, frameworks like LwM2M and SWUpdate have been standardized or widely adopted
in industrial and academic testbeds, while research prototypes continue to extend them with energy-aware
scheduling and secure bootstrapping for heterogeneous edge devices [224]. Together, these results highlight
that secure and eicient OTA updates are not only feasible on MCU-class devices but also critical to sustaining
maintainability across large-scale, heterogeneous edge deployments.

Federated, Continual, and Decentralized Updating. In scenarios where connectivity or privacy constraints limit
direct model replacement, federated learning provides a complementary strategy. Devices collaboratively update a
shared model by exchanging parameter deltas or gradients instead of raw data. Such approaches reduce bandwidth
usage and enhance privacy, though they introduce new challenges in heterogeneity, energy consumption, and
catastrophic forgetting. Over-the-air federated learning (OTA-FL) leverages the superposition property of wireless
multi-access channels to perform low-latency model aggregation, thereby alleviating spectrum bottlenecks in
large-scale edge deployments. Recent surveys highlight both its promise in reducing communication overhead
and the unique challenges of synchronization, fading, and privacy that distinguish OTA-FL from conventional
federated learning [265].
Continual learning provides a related mechanism for post-deployment adaptation, allowing models to in-
corporate progressively arriving data, respond to distribution drift, and reduce the need for full retraining. In
distributed edge settings, federated continual learning (FCL) combines privacy-preserving federated learning
with continual adaptation, making it suitable for dynamic environments where data distributions evolve and
clients may join or leave unpredictably [259].
Beyond model aggregation, decentralized principles are also being applied to the update dissemination process
itself. Oliveira et al. present an OTA framework for software-deined vehicles that combines mobile edge computing
with the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS) to deliver resilient updates under intermittent V2X connectivity. Real-
world trials in a smart-city living lab conirm that this design enables agile update distribution while mitigating
service disruption and reducing operational costs [183]. Complementary work by Malik et al. proposes a fog-
federated dissemination scheme in which pivot vehicles relay updates from fog nodes using V2V links, coordinated
through SDN controllers. Their evaluation reports approximately 40% improvement in convergence time and
95% trustworthiness against malicious-node scenarios, compared to prior fog-based schemes [162]. These results
indicate that decentralized update dissemination, whether for model retraining or binary delivery, remains a
promising yet underexplored strategy for large-scale, heterogeneous edge environments.
Challenges and Trends. Commercial IoT OTA frameworks, such as Balena [8], Mender [9], Particle Cloud [11],
provide secure phased rollouts and delta updates for irmware and application binaries. However, empirical
analyses of vendor practices conirm that such solutions remain predominantly artifact-centric, treating models
as opaque assets for replacement rather than exposing model-aware primitives [32, 38, 231]. Consequently,
ine-grained adaptation mechanisms, such as subgraph-level updates, shadow model deployment, or selective
operator replacement, are not yet present in mainstream OTA worklows [38]. Research prototypes such as
RIOT-ML demonstrate that partial OTA updates, like updating only speciic layers, can dramatically shrink
payloads. In their MCU case study, transmitting only the model instead of the full irmware reduced transfer
costs by more than 60%, while updating only the inal layer eliminated over 99% of the network load [103]. These
mechanisms remain early-stage: although RIOT-ML integrates uTVM to enable compilation and optimization,
broader adoption in production-grade OTA worklows is still limited. Similarly, adaptive recalibration, where
models are periodically ine-tuned with locally collected data to address concept drift, is highlighted in the
literature as a promising strategy for preserving accuracy [95]. These gaps suggest the need for modular OTA
pipelines that integrate with intermediate representations and deployment runtimes, allowing selective and
adaptive updates while balancing reliability, bandwidth, and energy.

While conventional ML evaluation metrics such as accuracy, F1-score, or mean Average Precision remain
necessary, they are insuicient for edge deployments, where feasibility is determined by a combination of accuracy,
latency, energy consumption, and resource constraints [24, 232]. Several aspects of deployment evaluation are
particularly important. Because these dimensions are interdependent, they should ultimately be interpreted
through a multi-objective lens rather than as isolated metrics.
Latency and Responsiveness. While many deployment studies report average inference time and throughput,
averages alone can be misleading in real-time and interactive contexts. In safety-critical control (e.g., UAV
navigation), even bounded bursts of deadline misses can destabilize the closed loop, as shown in modern weakly-
hard real-time control analyses [158]. More broadly, systems research has shown that rare but extreme latency
outliers dominate user-perceived responsiveness in large-scale services, motivating the use of tail-latency metrics
(e.g., 95th or 99th percentiles) rather than means alone [58]. Taken together, these perspectives emphasize that
deployment evaluations must consider worst-case and tail behaviors, not just averages, to capture the true
risks to stability and responsiveness. Relecting these insights, standardized ML benchmarks now emphasize
latency distributions rather than averages: MLPerf Inference requires percentile-based reporting for server- and
edge-class devices [201], while MLPerf Tiny extends standardized latency, energy, and accuracy reporting to
microcontrollers and embedded platforms [24].
Energy and Power Eiciency. Energy per inference (mJ/inference) provides a more deployment-relevant met-
ric than instantaneous power draw, as it directly relects device lifetime under battery constraints. However,
measurement methodologies remain heterogeneous, ranging from benchtop probes to on-chip counters, which
hinders comparability across studies. Tu et al. [248] address this gap by introducing kernel-level energy predictors
and standardized scoring metrics across diverse edge devices, ofering one of the irst systematic baselines for
energy benchmarking. Complementary work on embedded AI platforms demonstrates empirical energy models
for CNN inference on NVIDIA Jetson boards [133], while application-driven deployments, such as UAV TinyML
pipelines, highlight real-world energy-accuracy trade-ofs under strict power budgets [200].
Deployment-Aware Footprint. Model size and memory usage must be evaluated in the context of deployment,
not in isolation. OTA update pipelines are tightly constrained by binary size and the feasibility of lightweight
update mechanisms, a challenge emphasized in TinyMLOps operational studies [137]. Real-world IoT deployments
reveal that the severe Flash/RAM constraints of microcontroller-class devices hinder the adoption of conventional
reliability and security mechanisms, creating potential failure points in update and maintenance worklows [67].
Surveys of secure irmware update frameworks further highlight that even tiny metadata such as manifests or
cryptographic signatures can exceed RAM budgets on microcontroller-class systems, underscoring that Flash
and RAM requirements are truly deployment-limiting constraints [274]. Security overhead, discussed in the
OTA update subsection, complements deployment-aware footprint evaluation by quantifying the added latency,
energy consumption, memory usage, transmitted bytes, and installation time introduced by secure model-update
mechanisms.
Beyond Inference: End-to-End Pipeline Costs. Deployment evaluation should consider the complete sensing-
to-decision pipeline, rather than model execution alone. On constrained platforms, accelerated inference can
shift the main latency or energy cost to sensor processing, feature extraction, data movement, or CPU-side
post-processing. For example, Ulkar et al. [250] show that MFCC feature extraction on a Cortex-M4F MCU can
consume more energy than CNN inference on the MAX78000 accelerator. Recent TinyML benchmarking work
similarly separates pre-inference, inference, and post-inference phases to expose such non-inference costs [27].

prior observations that eicient model deployment involves Pareto trade-ofs between accuracy and real-world

provide a more informative view of the feasible design space and help practitioners choose conigurations that
match application-speciic constraints. In practice, Pareto-based reporting is most useful when conigurations
are measured under comparable datasets, hardware platforms, and evaluation protocols; otherwise, it should be
treated as a qualitative framework for interpreting trade-ofs rather than as a direct ranking method.

and lifecycle lexibility. Kernel fusion, operator scheduling, memory planning, and accelerator mapping can
reduce latency and energy consumption, but because they often specialize execution to particular tensor shapes,
memory hierarchies, and accelerator targets, they may also constrain lexibility after deployment [49, 136, 223,
253, 283]. For example, aggressive graph-level optimization may produce highly eicient hardware-speciic
execution plans while simultaneously making later adaptation, partial model replacement, or cross-platform
migration more diicult. Hardware-software co-design methods such as JAQ illustrate this interaction by jointly
optimizing network architecture, quantization precision, and accelerator mapping, thereby improving eiciency
while coupling the resulting deployment to speciic hardware assumptions [253]. This creates a potential trade-
of between deployment eiciency and lifecycle lexibility. In static applications, specialized binaries may be
preferable; in long-lived IoT deployments, however, maintainability and update granularity may be equally

and privacy, but it increases the need for compact models, local runtime monitoring, and secure OTA update

centralized resources, but they become sensitive to bandwidth, latency, and network availability. Split-inference

model structure, device heterogeneity, partitioning strategy, and network conditions [82, 101, 126]. Therefore,
connectivity is not only a deployment-stage concern; it should inluence earlier choices such as model size,
feature extraction strategy, compression level, and whether the system supports federated learning, adaptive

Monitoring, Drift Detection, and Update Mechanisms. Post-deployment monitoring, drift detection, and update
mechanisms depend on earlier architectural and deployment decisions. Monitoring can reveal performance drift,
anomalous behavior, or changing resource conditions, but acting on this information depends on earlier design
decisions [61, 95, 127, 170]. If the model is monolithic, updating it may require replacing a larger irmware image,
increasing OTA cost and rollback complexity. If the model and runtime are modular, only selected model binaries,
layers, parameters, or related components may need replacement. RIOT-ML illustrates this interaction by showing

such savings require earlier design choices that support modular model artifacts and compatible deployment
runtimes [103]. Similarly, monitoring itself consumes memory, energy, and communication resources, which
means that telemetry requirements should be considered during hardware selection and model design rather
than added only after deployment [103, 137, 231].
Lifecycle Evaluation. These cross-layer dependencies suggest that edge AI deployment should be evaluated using
multi-objective criteria that span the full lifecycle. Accuracy, latency, energy, memory footprint, portability, update
size, monitoring overhead, and security cost should be considered jointly rather than in isolation [24, 25, 201, 248].

Challenges and Future Directions
Despite signiicant advances in algorithms, deployment toolchains, and embedded computing platforms, scalable
and reliable edge AI deployment on resource-constrained devices remains constrained by several systemic
challenges. This section highlights the main recurring challenges that emerge from the literature and identiies
promising directions for future work.
Fragmentation and Portability. Edge AI deployment pipelines often consist of loosely connected stages for
model optimization, compilation, and runtime execution. This fragmentation reduces portability, as worklows
are frequently tied to speciic processor architectures, operator sets, and vendor libraries [24, 59]. The absence of
universally accepted intermediate representations and modular design principles prevents seamless interoperabil-
ity between toolchains. Future work must explore adaptive abstraction layers and standardized operator sets to
balance developer accessibility with transparent control over optimization trade-ofs [49, 136].
Consistent and Reproducible Evaluation. Performance evaluation in edge AI lacks methodological consistency.
Metrics such as latency, accuracy, and energy eiciency are reported under heterogeneous preprocessing pipelines
and experimental setups, limiting comparability across studies [24, 201]. While benchmark suites such as MLPerf
Tiny and MLMark provide standardized tasks and metrics, broader adoption of uniied evaluation frameworks
is needed to cover diverse workloads and end-to-end system behaviors. An open problem is to extend these
benchmarks toward multi-objective reporting (e.g., Pareto frontiers) that relect trade-ofs between latency, energy,
and accuracy.
Adaptive Deployment. While much prior work targets homogeneous and static system settings, real-world
edge deployments must operate under heterogeneous hardware, luctuating network conditions, limited power
budgets, and dynamically changing data distributions [101, 286]. Current methods for dynamic scheduling and
adaptive inference are fragmented across research prototypes and are rarely integrated into commercial toolchains.
Promising directions include runtime decision-making for model complexity and oloading strategies, as well as
training methods that embed deployment constraints directly into architecture design [232].
Security and Privacy. Edge deployments face growing risks to data privacy, model integrity, and resilience
against adversarial manipulation [67, 191, 231]. Security measures such as encrypted storage, secure boot, and OTA
integrity veriication are often implemented independently rather than through an integrated lifecycle. However,
quantization and device limitations can weaken the adversarial robustness of TinyML models, underscoring the
importance of addressing security and privacy consistently throughout the deployment process while operating
within tight energy and memory constraints.
Emerging Workloads. The evolution of edge AI workloads is reshaping the assumptions behind constrained-
device deployment. While many TinyML and embedded-AI systems remain centered on compact CNNs, keyword-
spotting models, image classiiers, and sensor classiiers [24, 209], recent progress in mobile vision transformers,
large language models, difusion and other generative models, multimodal learning, and vision-language-action
(VLA) models points toward workloads with diferent computational and memory proiles [47, 140, 164, 208, 214,

on latency, energy, privacy, and reliability.
Hardware-Software Co-Design for Emerging Edge AI. The growing complexity of edge workloads strengthens
the need for hardware-software co-design across model design, compiler optimization, runtime scheduling,
and accelerator execution. Compiler infrastructures and intermediate representations must support dynamic
execution patterns, variable input shapes, memory reuse, and heterogeneous partitioning across CPUs, GPUs,
NPUs, and other accelerators [49, 136, 187, 284]. At the hardware and runtime levels, these trends increase the
importance of tensor acceleration, low-precision arithmetic, memory-aware execution, energy-aware adaptation,
and accelerator-aware scheduling [23, 147, 166, 187, 253]. Recent systems show that edge deployment beneits
when these layers are optimized together: MCUNet co-designs neural architecture search with a memory-eicient
inference engine for microcontrollers, JAQ jointly explores network architecture, ultra-low-bit quantization,
compiler mapping, and accelerator architecture, while Protean combines heterogeneous battery-free hardware,
adaptive runtime scheduling, and code generation for intermittent ML execution [23, 144, 253]. Future deployment
pipelines should therefore move beyond treating model compression, operator lowering, memory planning,
accelerator mapping, and runtime scheduling as separate stages. This is especially important if edge AI is to
evolve from isolated compact inference tasks toward adaptive and interactive intelligence under constrained
power, memory, and connectivity budgets.
These challenges point to a common limitation in current edge AI deployment: optimization, compilation,
benchmarking, adaptation, and security are still often addressed as separate problems. A central direction for future
work is therefore to develop deployment worklows that can coordinate these concerns across heterogeneous
devices, evolving workloads, and long-term lifecycle requirements.

Conclusion
This survey reviewed edge AI deployment on constrained devices across optimization, toolchains, lifecycle
management, benchmarking, and applications. The literature shows strong progress in model compression,
hardware-aware execution, and deployment tooling, but weaker integration of portability, monitoring, secure
updates, runtime adaptation, and reproducible evaluation. This fragmentation remains a key barrier to scalable
edge AI deployment. Future pipelines should therefore move beyond isolated model or compiler optimizations
toward lifecycle-aware worklows that support eicient, observable, updatable, and secure operation across
heterogeneous devices.
